LIFE CYCLE COST & ASSESSMENT

Costing and Environmental Impact of Building Renovation Measures
Three linked coursework assignments: costing renovation options for a 1930s church, comparing insulation materials by embodied impact, then fusing both methods into one decision poster
  • Location Lund University, LTH — Division of Energy and Building Design
  • Course ABKN05 — Life Cycle Perspective and Environmental Assessment of Buildings
  • Team members Vinay Dilip Jayaselan,Pelin Ilkyaz, Joel John Panikulangara
  • Term Autumn 2025
PDF Assignment 1 PDF Assignment 2 PPT LCC Slides PDF LCA Poster

Three assignments, one question asked three different ways: what does a renovation choice actually cost once you account for its full life cycle — in money, and in environmental impact? Assignment 1 ran a Life Cycle Cost (LCC) analysis on two energy-saving measures for Rönnekyrkan, a 1930s church in Ängelholm, Sweden. Assignment 2 stepped back to a materials question — comparing mineral wool against XPS insulation for a different building using full Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Assignment 3 brought both methods back together, adding a Dutch shadow-cost layer to monetise the church's environmental impacts directly alongside its financial ones.

ASSIGNMENT 1 — LCC OF ENERGY MEASURES

Group 9 — Pelin Ilkyaz, Joel John Panikulangara, Vinay Dilip Jayaselan. For Rönnekyrkan's hot water system, sensor taps alone (Option 1) cut annual water consumption 73% (12,294 L to 3,299 L) for a 6,500 SEK investment, but the payback period runs to 34.2 years; adding an electric water heater (Option 2) achieved the same water savings but was never cost-competitive once its electricity demand was factored in. For the front door, the story flipped: an automatic door (Door 3, 50,000 SEK) paid back in just 2.38 years and cut lifecycle costs 61% versus the existing door, making it the clearer financial win of the two measures studied.

ASSIGNMENT 2 — COMPARATIVE LCA OF INSULATION MATERIALS

1 / 5

Group 6 — Stanislav Rosina, Tural Doruk Aral, Vlad Gavril Marchis, Vinay Dilip Jayaselan. A different building this time — "Futura Haus neun," a single-family house near Lund — used to compare mineral wool against XPS as external wall insulation, from 0 to 500 mm in 50 mm steps, over a 50-year lifetime. For mineral wool, the operational heating energy dominates both ozone depletion (ODP) and fossil depletion (ADP) more than the material itself does; for XPS, it's reversed — the material's own embodied impact swamps the energy savings it enables. The practical answer: 200–350 mm of mineral wool balances embodied and operational impact best, while XPS's high ODP holds up even at low thicknesses, which is hard to justify against that comparison.

ASSIGNMENT 3 — INTEGRATED LCC + LCA POSTER

Solo assignment. Returning to Rönnekyrkan, this poster fuses Assignment 1's LCC method with a full LCA and a Dutch shadow-cost layer that converts environmental impacts (ADP-fossil, ODP, GWP, AP, EP, water) into a single monetised figure, letting cost and environmental impact be compared on the same axis for the first time.

1 / 4

The base case carries the highest ADP-fossil impact by a wide margin. District heating with fixture replacement cuts that impact significantly, but the electric water heater with fixture replacement performs best across nearly every category — GWP, AP and EP terrestrial included. Once monetised through Dutch shadow costing, that same option (Op1) lands with the lowest external costs, while the option combining district heating and higher water use (Op3) comes out most expensive, driven almost entirely by ADP-fossil and water impacts.

TAKEAWAYS

  • Financial payback diverges from environmental value. The fixture retrofit takes 34 years for financial recovery, yet shadow costing proves it delivers the best lifecycle environmental and monetized value overall.
  • Short payback does not guarantee long-term economy. Higher upfront investment options often yield superior 20-year total lifecycle costs compared to lower-cost, shorter-payback alternatives.
  • Dominant emissions vary by insulation material. Mineral wool impacts stem primarily from operational energy, whereas XPS impacts are dominated by upfront embodied carbon, invalidating assumptions that all extra insulation is greener.
  • Shadow costing resolves multi-criteria ambiguity. Monetizing diverse LCA indicators into a singular financial unit allows direct comparison across trade-offs without subjective weighting.
  • Conclusions remain robust under sensitivity checks. Testing varying discount rates and future energy cost trajectories confirmed the long-term viability of the recommended renovation pathways.
