VENTILATION SYSTEM DESIGN

Design of a Ventilation System for an Office Building
A complete CAV ventilation system for a one-story Gothenburg office — energy simulation, ductwork, diffusers and air handling unit, designed as one system
  • Location Lund University, LTH — Department of Energy and Building Design
  • Course ABKN01 — Ventilation and Air Conditioning Systems
  • Team members Vinay Dilip Jayaselan, Lukas Rosberg, Sakineh J. Z. Afshari,
PDF Full Report

A one-story office in Gothenburg needed a constant-air-volume ventilation system sized around what the building actually needed for cooling, not just a rule of thumb. Rhino, Grasshopper and ClimateStudio were used to simulate peak cooling loads and thermal comfort, which set the airflow the whole downstream system — ducts, diffusers, and the air handling unit — had to be sized around.

The base-case building overheated badly: indoor operative temperature exceeded 26°C for 51% of hours between April and September. Shading, better glazing, more efficient lighting, a shifted cooling schedule and 80% sensible heat recovery brought that down to 10% before a single duct was sized.

SYSTEM OVERVIEW

The building is modelled as a single open-plan office with an all-air CAV system: one air handling unit on the roof, feeding supply and exhaust ducts down through a central shaft to diffusers positioned over every workstation. Reducing peak cooling load first — through shading, glazing and lighting changes — meant the downstream ductwork and AHU could be sized smaller and cheaper than a base-case design would have needed.

DUCT SYSTEM

Circular ducts were used throughout — cheaper, more airtight and easier to install than rectangular sections. Each duct was sized against a friction-loss chart, then checked for air velocity and pressure drop, including fittings via their equivalent lengths. Supply and exhaust runs both connect back to the AHU through the building's main shaft, which kept the layout close to symmetrical even though the furniture plan and shaft position ruled out a perfectly mirrored design.

DIFFUSERS

1 / 2

The EAGLE CRa 315-600-RO + ALSd 250-315 (Swegon) was selected for both supply and exhaust, chosen to avoid draught, keep CO₂ concentration in an acceptable range, and support CAV operation. A 0.9 m ceiling void limited which diffuser connection types would fit, ruling out top-connected models in favour of diffusers with side connections.

AIR HANDLING UNIT & ENERGY

A total airflow of 1.9 m³/s across the building set the AHU size: size 30 was selected for a final air velocity of 2.35 m/s, comfortably under the 2.5 m/s design limit, with a measured temperature efficiency of 83% on the heat exchanger. Specific fan power came out inside the 1.0–2.0 kW/(m³/s) range considered efficient for this class of system.

1 / 5

Coil sizing used the Gothenburg EPW design extremes — -15.9°C in winter, 27.1°C in summer — traced through a Mollier diagram. With 83% heat recovery covering most of the heating load, the preheating and heating coils came out at 4.58 kW and 19.24 kW respectively; the cooling coil, sized against the summer design condition down to an 18°C supply temperature, came out at 32.83 kW.

TAKEAWAYS

  • Upstream cooling reductions downscale mechanical infrastructure. Lowering peak cooling loads dropped overheating hours from 51% to 10%, directly downsizing downstream ductwork, chillers, and air handling equipment[cite: 8].
  • Practical layout constraints override theoretical symmetry. Shaft locations and desk configurations made strict symmetrical duct branching unfeasible, necessitating standardized duct diameters across zones[cite: 8].
  • Ceiling void depth acts as a hard boundary condition. The restricted 0.9 m plenum ruled out top-entry diffusers, requiring side-connection terminals to accommodate vertical space limits[cite: 8].
  • Design efficiency depends on baseline degradation factors. Sizing assumptions like 83% heat exchanger efficiency represent initial performance metrics that degrade over continuous operation[cite: 8].
  • Critical path optimization refines fan power performance. Although the system met the 1.0–2.0 kW/(m³/s) specific fan power target, streamlining the longest critical run provides further pressure reduction gains[cite: 8].
